# Doqment.in — Content Strategy & Website Copy (v3)
Prepared as a senior UX/content strategy pass: narrative arc, section copy, and the reasoning behind each choice. This version folds in three changes from earlier rounds: humanised copy with no em dashes, structural fixes based on the AIDA-per-block framework, and a broadened audience (freelancers first, agencies and small businesses second) covered on a single page.

---

## 1. The Positioning (one line)

> **"Know what your project needs, before it costs you."**

This line has to work for a freelancer taking a single client project, an agency owner running several projects through a small team, and a small business owner writing their first few contracts without a legal hire yet. It holds because the core promise, knowing what's missing before it becomes a problem, is the same fear across all three. What changes underneath it is the scenario, not the promise.

Freelancers stay the lead audience. They get the first and most detailed scenario in every section. Agencies and small businesses get real, specific mentions, not an afterthought line, but they come second.

---

## 2. The Strategic Frame

**The real enemy isn't disorganization. It's not seeing the risk coming, for any of these three.**

- A **freelancer** feels this on one project going wrong: a client stops responding after final delivery, and there was never a signed scope to point to.
- An **agency owner** feels this across a team: one account manager remembers to send an NDA, another doesn't, and consistency, not individual carelessness, becomes the risk.
- A **small business owner** feels this earlier and quieter: contracts have been happening over email and WhatsApp because there's no one in-house to say otherwise, and it only becomes a problem the first time a client disputes an invoice or asks for something in writing that was never written down.

Because pricing is pay per document at ₹19 or ₹4, none of these three audiences needs heavy trust machinery before the ask. The real question in each of their heads isn't "will I lose money if this goes wrong," it's "will this actually know what my situation needs, and will the document hold up." Credibility should come from precision (GST compliant, reasoning shown, per-project completeness) rather than generic trust badges.

---

## 3. Narrative Arc (page flow)

A structural note before the table: each block below should carry its own small attention, interest, desire, action arc rather than the page as a whole being divided into one attention block, one interest block, and so on. Treating AIDA as four separate sections in sequence is the single most common mistake in landing page structure, and it flattens momentum instead of building it. Every block should also end with a small pull toward the next one (anticipation), and objections should be resolved close to where they naturally arise rather than batched into one FAQ at the end.

| # | Section | Job it does |
|---|---------|--------------|
| 1 | Hero | Pattern interrupt, name the blind spot, promise the fix, free first step |
| 2 | The Real Cost | Three short scenarios (freelancer, agency, small business), each landing the same fear differently |
| 3 | How Guidance Works | The core mechanic, shown through one concrete example, with a nod to team and early-business use |
| 4 | From Guidance to Done | Generate and store, with the legal-advice reassurance seeded here, not deferred |
| 5 | Pricing | Reframe ₹19 against stakes, note that usage-based pricing scales naturally for agencies handling many projects |
| 6 | Why Trust This | Competence signals, honest about who this was built for first |
| 7 | FAQ | Only what's left unresolved: country support, roadmap, data, agency and small-business specific doubts |
| 8 | Final CTA | Smallest possible ask, phrased to include all three audiences without diluting it |

## 4. Section by Section Copy

### 1. Hero

**Headline (pattern interrupt, honest, not hyped):**
> "We didn't build a smarter template library. We built the checklist nobody hands you when you start a project."

**Sub-headline (the clean, searchable promise):**
> Know what your project needs, before it costs you.

**Subhead body:**
> Whether you're a freelancer taking on a new client, an agency running several projects at once, or a small business writing your first few contracts without a legal team yet, Doqment tells you exactly which documents you need, and why, before you're three weeks in and realising you skipped one.

**CTA:** `Check what my project needs, free`
*(Smaller line underneath: "No signup for the checklist. Takes about 30 seconds.")*

Why this works: the pattern-interrupt headline does two jobs, it earns a second look by not sounding like every other SaaS opener, and it's also a small, honest confession, which builds trust faster than a polished claim would. The subhead then does the work of widening the audience without losing the freelancer as the lead example.

---

### 2. The Real Cost, three scenarios, one fear

**Header:** "It's never the client. It's the paperwork nobody thought to write down."

**Freelancer (lead scenario, now a real, specific case rather than a hypothetical):**
> A freelancer takes on a ₹35,000 logo design and branding project. No Proposal, no SOW, just a verbal understanding of "the branding." A few weeks in, the client keeps adding: a few extra logo variations, then brand guidelines, then social media templates, all "part of branding, right?" There was nothing written down to say otherwise, so the freelancer ends up doing significantly more work, inside the same ₹35,000, because there was never a document defining where the project actually ended.

**Agency (secondary, also a real case):**
> An agency takes on social media management and Meta ad management for a client, a ₹1,00,000 contract. The understanding was that ad spend sits outside that ₹1,00,000, the client pays Meta directly or reimburses it separately. That was never written into anything the client signed. When the ad spend bill came due, the client pointed back to the ₹1,00,000 and said it should already be covered. Nothing on paper said otherwise, so the agency ended up paying the ad spend out of its own pocket to keep the campaigns running.

**Small business (secondary):**
> The first few clients happened over email, because there was no one around yet to say "we should probably get this in writing." It works fine until the first dispute, when there's nothing on paper to point to and no time to figure out what should have existed from day one.

**Closing line (anticipation hook into the next section):**
> None of this happens because anyone did something wrong. It happens because nobody told them what to have ready before they needed it. A Proposal and an SOW would have settled, in writing, exactly what "branding" meant on that ₹35,000 project, before the extra work started. The same goes for the agency's ₹1,00,000 contract, one line in an SOW stating ad spend is billed separately would have settled it before a single rupee came out of anyone's own pocket. So what actually decides which documents a project needs? That's the part every template site skips.

Why this works: three short, specific vignettes let each audience recognise themselves without the page reading as three separate pitches stitched together. The closing line does double duty, it reframes the problem as systemic rather than personal (removes shame, builds trust), and it pulls the reader forward instead of letting the section end flat.

### 3. How Guidance Works, the differentiator

**Header:** "You don't need to know contract law. You need to know what's missing."

**The concrete example, picking up the ₹35,000 branding project from the section above:**
> This time, before starting, the freelancer tells Doqment the client, the project type (branding), and the value (₹35,000). In seconds, back comes a ranked checklist: a **Proposal** (essential, because scope isn't agreed yet), an **SOW** (essential, because "branding" needs to be spelled out into exactly what's included and what isn't), a **Contract** (recommended, given the project value), and an **Invoice** (essential). Each one comes with a one-line reason why it matters for this specific project. Check them off, and watch the completeness meter fill up. The scope-creep that ate into that ₹35,000 the first time around never gets a chance to happen the second time.

**Same logic, for the agency's ₹1,00,000 social media and Meta ad management contract:**
> The SOW that Doqment ranks as essential here comes with a specific reason attached: define what's included in the ₹1,00,000 fee and what isn't, so a line stating "ad spend billed separately, reimbursed by client" exists before the first ad ever runs. The agency doesn't need to remember to think of this themselves, the checklist already knows this kind of engagement calls for it.

**Supporting line, widening without losing the thread:**
> Run this on every client project, every time, and it works the same way whether you're doing it solo, running it across a small team where consistency is the hard part, or setting it up for the first time because your business hasn't needed formal paperwork until now.

Why this works: leading with one fully worked, specific example (a named project value, named documents, named reasoning) does more for believability than a general description of "guidance" ever could. The widening line after it folds in agencies and small businesses without needing a second full example, which keeps the section tight.

### 4. From Guidance to Done, generation and storage (the retention loop)

**Header:** "Once you know what you need, get it done without leaving the page."

> Generate the actual document, pre-filled from your project details. GST compliant for India, standard compliant for the US. Invoices get automatic numbering and status tracking, so you always know what's a draft, what's been sent, what's paid, and what's overdue.
>
> A quick, honest note: this isn't legal advice, Doqment helps you generate the right documents and explains why they matter. But every document template behind it has been reviewed and approved by our lawyer before it ever reaches you, so what you're generating isn't a guess dressed up as a contract, it's a properly vetted document filled in with your project's details.
>
> Everything lives under the project it belongs to, so a growing agency or a small business scaling past its first few clients isn't stuck digging through email threads when something needs to be found six months later.

Why this works: the legal-advice caveat is seeded here, right where someone is about to generate a real document, instead of being saved for a FAQ they may never scroll to. Addressing it honestly, in plain language, builds more trust than a legal disclaimer buried at the bottom of the page.

### 5. Pricing, framed as confidence, not a hedge

**Header:** "Pay for what you generate. Nothing more."

> No subscription. No plan you don't need. Just:
> - Proposal, Contract, SOW: starting at ₹19 per document
> - Invoice: starting at ₹4
>
> That's less than the cost of a cup of chai to protect a project worth thousands. And because it's pay-per-document, an agency running a handful of projects a month simply pays for what it generates, never for seats or a tier it doesn't need.

**For agencies and small businesses generating documents in real volume:**
> Running a large number of client accounts or need documents in bulk? The same per-document pricing applies as standard, but past a certain volume, it's worth talking to us directly so we can work out pricing that actually fits how much you're generating. `Contact Sales` and someone from the team will get back to you within 24 to 48 hours.

*(Form fields: name, email, company name, which documents are needed, and roughly how many in total. This lets a human quote custom pricing rather than forcing a bulk buyer through the same ₹19-at-a-time flow a solo freelancer uses.)*

Why this works: the chai line still does the emotional anchoring for freelancers, and the standard-pricing-plus-contact-sales structure gives agencies a real answer without inventing a tier system this early, or making a solo freelancer wade through pricing complexity that was never meant for them.

### 6. Why Trust This, competence signals, honestly scoped

**Header:** "Built for freelancers first. Growing with agencies and small teams."

> Doqment started with a simple, specific problem: freelancers taking on client work without knowing what paperwork they'd need until it was too late. That same blind spot shows up in small agencies and early-stage businesses, just at a slightly different scale, so the same guidance engine works for all three.
>
> GST compliant invoicing for India, standard compliant invoicing for the US, and a system that explains why each document matters, not just that it exists.

Why this works: naming who it was built for first, rather than claiming to be everything for everyone from day one, reads as more credible for a new brand, not less. It also sets up honest expectations for agencies and small businesses reading this as secondary audiences.

### 7. FAQ

- **What if my country isn't India or the US?** It's in the pipeline. Drop us your requirement and we'll look at building support for it, we believe in building around what our customers actually need, not just what's easiest for us to ship first.
- **What happens after Proposal, Contract, SOW, and Invoice?** Coming soon: NDA and payment reminders are next up.
- **Are these documents legally binding, or checked by anyone qualified?** Every template is reviewed and approved by our lawyer before it's made available. Doqment itself isn't giving legal advice, it's handing you a properly vetted document filled in with your project details.
- **Can an agency use this across multiple client accounts?** Yes, standard per-document pricing applies the same way it does for a solo freelancer. If you're generating documents in real volume across many accounts, reach out through Contact Sales and someone from the team will respond within 24 to 48 hours with pricing that fits your actual usage.
- **I'm just starting my business, is this overkill for me?** Start with just the documents you need for your very first client. There's no plan to commit to and nothing to set up in advance, Doqment scales with you as your business grows, not the other way around.
- **Is my project data stored safely, and can I delete it if I want to?** Yes. Everything is encrypted and private, no one but you can access your documents. You can delete any document whenever you want, and if you'd rather not think about it at all, there's an optional disposable documents feature that automatically deletes documents for you after a set time.

## 5. Voice and Tone Guardrails

- Write the way one small business owner would explain this to another, not the way a SaaS company explains itself to a market.
- Avoid words like "AI powered," "revolutionary," or "game changing." Precision and honesty are the trust play here, not hype.
- Seed reassurance where the doubt naturally occurs (legal advice near document generation, no-subscription near pricing) rather than saving every objection for one FAQ block at the bottom.
- Every section should end with something that pulls the reader forward, even a single line, rather than closing flat.
- Keep sentences short. This audience reads on a phone, often between client calls.
